Facts, not fears

Nuclear myths, explained

Many people worry about nuclear energy because of what they have heard, not what scientists have measured. Here are the most common myths, and what the evidence actually shows.

First, two words to know

Radiation is measured in two main ways. Knowing them makes the numbers on this page easy to follow.

Becquerel (Bq)

How radioactive something is: one atom changing (decaying) every second.

Sievert (Sv)

The dose: how much radiation the body receives, and its possible effect. It is a big unit, so we use smaller ones:

mSv and µSv

1 millisievert (mSv) = one thousandth of a sievert. 1 microsievert (µSv) = one millionth. 1 mSv = 1,000 µSv.

1

The banana test

Bananas are rich in potassium, which our bodies need. A tiny part of all natural potassium is potassium-40, which is radioactive. So every banana is slightly radioactive: about 13 atoms decay inside it every second.

Eating one banana gives a dose of roughly 0.1 µSv. Scientists even joke about a "banana equivalent dose" to help people picture tiny amounts of radiation.

Your own body holds around 4,000 Bq of natural potassium-40, plus some natural carbon-14. Every person on Earth is a little radioactive, and always has been.

Good to knowEating more bananas does not build up radiation in you: the body keeps its potassium level steady and removes the extra. The banana is simply a handy way to picture very small doses.
2

Radiation is natural: meet NORM

NORM stands for Naturally Occurring Radioactive Material. Radioactive elements such as uranium, thorium and potassium have been in the Earth's rocks, soil, water and air since the planet formed. We are surrounded by them every day.

Rocks and soil

Granite and many soils contain natural uranium and thorium. Rwanda's own soils and rocks do too, as everywhere on Earth.

Radon gas

A natural gas that seeps up from the ground into buildings. It is the biggest source of natural radiation for most people.

Food

Bananas, potatoes, beans and Brazil nuts all contain natural radioactive elements. They are all safe and healthy to eat.

Space and altitude

Cosmic rays from space reach us all the time. Higher places get a little more. Kigali, at about 1,500 m, gets slightly more than cities at sea level.

Ten common myths

MythA nuclear power plant can explode like a nuclear bomb.
Fact

It is physically impossible. Power reactor fuel contains only about 3–5% uranium-235; a bomb needs around 90%. The fuel simply cannot explode like a bomb.

IAEA

MythAll radiation is man-made and deadly.
Fact

Most radiation we receive is natural, from rocks, radon, food and space. What matters is the dose. Small doses, like the ones on the ladder above, are part of everyday life.

UNSCEAR

MythLiving near a nuclear power plant exposes you to dangerous radiation.
Fact

People living near a normally operating plant typically receive less than 1% of their natural background dose from it. Limits for the public are set far below levels known to cause harm, and the regulator monitors them.

US NRC; ICRP 103

MythNuclear is the most dangerous way to make electricity.
Fact

Counting accidents and air pollution, nuclear is among the safest sources, on a par with wind and solar, and hundreds of times safer than coal. See the chart.

Our World in Data

MythNuclear power pollutes the air and heats the planet.
Fact

Nothing burns in a reactor. Over its whole life, nuclear power emits about 12 g of CO₂ per kWh, similar to wind and far below gas (about 490 g) or coal (about 820 g).

IPCC AR5

MythNuclear waste is a huge mountain we can't manage.
Fact

Used fuel is solid and small in volume. All the used fuel the United States has produced since the 1950s would fit on a single football field, stacked less than 10 yards high. It is stored in sealed, monitored containers.

US Department of Energy

MythA nuclear plant will make our lakes radioactive.
Fact

The water used for cooling never touches the fuel: it runs in separate, closed loops. Any releases are strictly limited and monitored by the regulator, which in Rwanda is RURA.

IAEA safety standards

MythFood treated with radiation becomes radioactive.
Fact

No. Food irradiation kills germs and insects, like an X-ray passing through luggage, and does not make food radioactive. It is approved by the WHO and FAO and helps reduce food waste.

IAEA; WHO; FAO

MythNuclear energy is too complex for countries like Rwanda.
Fact

The IAEA's step-by-step Milestones Approach guides newcomer countries. Its 2026 review found Rwanda had made significant progress. Small modular reactors are designed to suit smaller grids like Rwanda's.

IAEA INIR mission, March 2026

MythA nuclear power programme means nuclear weapons.
Fact

Rwanda's programme is for peaceful use only. Rwanda is party to the Non-Proliferation Treaty and has a safeguards agreement and Additional Protocol with the IAEA (INFCIRC/801), which lets inspectors verify that nuclear material is used peacefully.

IAEA; RAEB

What about Chernobyl and Fukushima?Both were serious accidents and the industry learned hard lessons from them. Chernobyl was an old Soviet design without a strong containment building, operated during an unsafe test. At Fukushima, the UN scientific committee (UNSCEAR) found no adverse health effects among residents that can be directly attributed to radiation; the evacuation itself caused real harm. Modern designs, including SMRs, add passive safety systems that cool the reactor without power or human action.
