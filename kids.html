The tiny atom with big power

What is nuclear energy, how does it make electricity, and why is Rwanda planning its own nuclear power plant? Let's find out together.

1

Everything is made of atoms

Your pencil, the water you drink, the air and even you are made of tiny pieces called atoms. They are so small that a single grain of sand has more atoms than there are grains of sand on all the beaches in the world.

In the middle of every atom is a nucleus. It is made of protons and neutrons squeezed tightly together. Even tinier electrons zoom around the outside.

The word nuclear simply means "about the nucleus".

2

Splitting an atom makes heat

Some atoms are very big and a little wobbly. One of them is called uranium. When a tiny neutron hits a uranium nucleus, the nucleus splits into two smaller pieces.

This splitting is called fission. It gives off lots of heat and also shoots out two or three new neutrons. Those neutrons can hit more uranium atoms, which split too. This is a chain reaction.

In a power plant, special control rods soak up extra neutrons, like a brake, so the chain reaction stays steady and calm.

Wow!One small uranium fuel pellet, about the size of the tip of your finger, gives about as much energy as a tonne of coal.
3

From heat to the light in your room

A nuclear power plant works a lot like a giant kettle. The heat from splitting atoms boils water. The steam pushes a big wheel with blades, called a turbine, and makes it spin. The spinning turbine turns a generator, which makes electricity. Wires carry the electricity to homes, schools, hospitals and factories.

No smoke

Nothing burns inside a nuclear power plant, so it does not puff out smoke or the gases that make the planet warmer. The white clouds you sometimes see are only water vapour.

Day and night

The sun sets and rain stops, but a nuclear plant can keep making electricity all day and all night, in every season.

Tiny fuel

Because a little uranium gives so much energy, a nuclear plant needs only a small amount of fuel. New fuel is added only every year or two.

4

How do we keep it safe?

Nuclear fuel gives off something invisible called radiation. A little radiation is all around us every day, from rocks, the ground and even the sky. Too much can be harmful, so nuclear plants keep it locked inside.

The fuel sits inside layer after layer of protection: strong metal tubes, a thick steel container and a very thick concrete building. Plants also have many ways to shut down safely and keep cool.

Highly trained people run the plant, and in Rwanda a regulator called RURA acts like a referee: it checks that all the safety rules are followed. The IAEA, a world organisation, helps countries learn from each other.

Used fuel is a small amount of solid material. It is stored carefully in strong containers, away from people.

5

Nuclear science helps in other ways too

Nuclear science is not only for electricity. Rwanda wants to use it in many parts of life.

Hospitals

Doctors use nuclear medicine to take pictures inside the body and to treat illnesses like cancer.

Farms

Scientists use it to grow stronger crops, keep food fresh for longer and stop harmful insects.

Science and training

Rwanda's planned Centre for Nuclear Science and Technology will have a small research reactor for science, training and making medicines.

Industry

Factories use it to check that pipes and machines are strong and safe without breaking them.

6

Why does Rwanda want nuclear power?

Rwanda is growing fast. More homes, schools, hospitals and factories all need electricity. In 2024 the country needed up to 242 megawatts at its busiest moment. If that keeps growing by about 10% every year, Rwanda could need around 12 times as much by 2050.

Today Rwanda's electricity comes mostly from methane gas taken from Lake Kivu, from water flowing through rivers (hydropower), from neighbouring countries, from peat and from the sun.

Nuclear power would add a strong, clean source that works day and night, rain or shine.

Sources: Least Cost Power Development Plan 2024 Review (REG, 2025); REG, 2026

7

A small reactor for a small country

Some nuclear power plants in the world are enormous. Rwanda plans to use a Small Modular Reactor, or SMR.

Small because it makes less power than a giant plant, which suits Rwanda's electricity network. Modular because its parts can be built in a factory, like big building blocks, and then put together on site. More blocks can be added later as Rwanda needs more electricity.

Rwanda's plan suggests a first unit of about 55 megawatts, which could start in the early 2030s.

8

Rwanda's nuclear journey

Building a nuclear power plant takes many years of careful preparation. Here are the big steps so far, and what comes next.

2019
Agreement for a science centre

Rwanda agreed with Russia to build a Centre for Nuclear Science and Technology with a research reactor.

2020
RAEB is created

The Rwanda Atomic Energy Board was set up to lead the peaceful use of nuclear energy in Rwanda.

2025
Students can study nuclear science

The University of Rwanda and RAEB launched a Bachelor's degree in Nuclear Science and Technology.

2026
A check-up from world experts

The IAEA visited Rwanda to review how ready the country is for nuclear power, and later for the research reactor. They found strong progress.

Next
Studies, laws and choosing a site

Rwanda will finish its nuclear law, study the best place for the plant and choose the technology.

Early 2030s
The first small reactor

Rwanda's goal is for its first SMR to start making electricity.

9

You could work in nuclear one day!

Rwanda will need many skilled people to build and run its nuclear programme. The young people in school today could become:

Engineer

Designs, builds and runs the reactor, turbines and power lines.

Scientist

Studies atoms and finds new ways to use nuclear science.

Safety inspector

Checks that everything follows the safety rules, like a referee.

Nuclear medicine doctor

Uses nuclear science to find and treat illnesses.

New words

Atom
A tiny building block that everything is made of.
Nucleus
The centre of an atom, made of protons and neutrons.
Uranium
A heavy metal from the ground, used as nuclear fuel.
Fission
When a big nucleus splits into two, giving off heat.
Reactor
The part of a power plant where fission happens safely.
Megawatt (MW)
A unit for power. 1 MW is enough to light about 100,000 LED bulbs at the same time.
SMR
Small Modular Reactor: a smaller nuclear reactor built from factory-made parts.
RAEB
Rwanda Atomic Energy Board, which leads Rwanda's nuclear programme.
RURA
Rwanda's regulator, which checks that nuclear activities are safe.
IAEA
International Atomic Energy Agency, which helps countries use nuclear energy safely and peacefully.
